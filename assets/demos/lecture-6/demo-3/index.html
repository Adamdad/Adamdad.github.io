    01The dilemma

    Good is slow. Fast is sloppy.

    A large model is a careful writer, but it writes one word per call and every call is expensive. A model ten times smaller answers ten times quicker — yet it is not the writer we asked for. Press Write and watch both.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    A GPU does thousands of things at once. Why can’t the big model produce all 20 words in a single call?

    Each word needs the previous ones. Word 5 is chosen by reading words 1 to 4, so it cannot be computed before word 4 exists. Writing is a chain: 20 words, 20 calls, one after another — even with a KV cache.

    Takeaway

    We want the big model’s words at something like the small model’s speed.

    Most words are easy.

    Here are the big model’s 20 words again. At every position we ask the small model for its first guess. Green means it would have guessed the very same word; red means it would have said something else. Click a word.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    Most of them — 15 of 20 here. After “sat on” almost any model says “the”; after “mat” comes a full stop. The two models only part ways at a few real forks, such as cat or dog.

    3
    03A second lucky fact

    Checking is faster than writing.

    To write word 4, the big model must wait for word 3. But if the words are already on the page — guessed by someone else — nothing has to wait: the model can read them all in one call and say, for every position, what it would have written there.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    Five guessed words are already on the page. How many big-model calls does it take to check all five?

    One. Every position only needs to read the words before it, and they are all there. It is the same reason a long prompt is processed in a single pass (prefill in the KV Cache Lab).
    Tick the clock
    Writingeach word waits for the one before it
    0big-model calls
    Checkingthe guessed words are already on the page
    0big-model calls

    One tick is one call of the big model. A call that looks at a handful of positions takes about as long as a call that looks at one — the GPU handles them side by side.

    Takeaway

    Writing n words takes n calls. Checking n words takes one.

    04The idea in one picture

    Guess, check, keep.

    Two names first. The big model is the target: its text is what we want. The small one is the draft model. One round has three moves — and from here on we say token for a word or word piece. Press Next round and count how often each lane calls the target.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    The draft model proposed most of the tokens. Whose writing is the final text?

    Exactly the target’s. The draft only decides which tokens get proposed. The target has the last word on every one of them, and its own token replaces the first miss. Part III shows the rule that makes this exact — not merely close.

    05Question 1 · one pass

    One pass gives the target’s view at every position.

    Call the number of drafted tokens γ. The draft samples each of them from its own distribution q. One target pass then returns the target’s distribution p at all γ positions — plus one more, for the token after them. Click a position to compare the two models.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    The check at position 3 was computed assuming guesses 1 and 2 are right. Guess 2 turns out to be wrong. Is the check at position 3 still usable?

    No. p₃ was computed after reading x₁ and x₂. Once x₂ is replaced, everything after it was conditioned on a token that is no longer in the text. That is why a round always stops at the first rejection.
    Click a position
    draft qtarget p
    Takeaway

    γ cheap draft calls, then one target pass that returns γ + 1 distributions.

    6
    06Question 2 · the accept rule

    Keep a guess as often as the target agrees.

    In Part I, “agree” meant “same first guess”. But models roll dice, so agreement comes in degrees: the draft proposes a token with probability q, and the target would have chosen it with probability p. One position, two possible tokens — drag both and press Draw.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    The draft proposes A with q = 0.8, but the target only gives A p = 0.6. What should we do with a proposed A?

    Some of the time — with probability p / q = 0.75. The draft proposes A too often, so we thin it out by exactly the right factor. B is proposed too rarely (q = 0.2 < p = 0.4), so a proposed B is always kept.

    both agreedraft too eagerdraft too shy
    Takeaway

    Accept a draft token with probability min(1, p / q).

    07Question 3 · the correction

    Corrections go where the draft was too shy.

    After a rejection we must still output a token. Sample it from the residual — the part of the target’s probability the draft did not cover (purple). Switch the rule and compare what comes out with the target.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    After a rejection, why not simply sample the replacement from the target p?

    It double-counts. Accepted draws already supplied min(p, q) for every token. Adding a plain sample from p on top gives too much to tokens the draft likes. Only the missing part, max(0, p − q), must be added back — try the middle button.

    Accept + residual correction reproduces the target distribution exactly.

    08Question 4 · a full round

    Keep the prefix, correct once, drop the rest.

    Now the three moves from Part II with real numbers. Check the draft tokens left to right: everything before the first rejection is kept, the rejected position is corrected from the residual, and later drafts are thrown away because their context is now wrong.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    All γ = 4 draft tokens are accepted. How many tokens does this round add to the text?

    Five. The same target pass already produced the distribution for the position after the last draft token, so one more token is sampled from it for free. A round always commits between 1 and γ + 1 tokens.

    Set the temperature to 0, so the target always picks its single top token. What does line 10 turn into?

    An exact-match test. At temperature 0 the target puts probability 1 on one token and 0 on all others, so p / q is at least 1 for that token and 0 for the rest. The rule becomes “keep the guess if it is what the target would have picked” — the Part I intuition is the special case.

    10Question 5 · when does drafting save time?

    Tokens per round, divided by cost per round.

    A round costs γ draft calls plus one target call, and returns a few tokens. Three numbers decide whether that beats standard decoding: the acceptance rate α, the draft’s relative cost c, and the draft length γ.

    ✎ Pause & predict

    The draft is accepted 60% of the time and each draft call costs 30% of a target call. With γ = 5, is drafting faster?

    Slower. A round yields about 2.4 tokens but costs 5 × 0.3 + 1 = 2.5 target-call units: 0.95×. A sloppy or expensive draft can make things worse — set α = 0.60 and c = 0.30 and look below the dashed line.
    Find the break-even

    Analytic model of Leviathan, Kalman & Matias (2023): acceptances are treated as independent with rate α; one verification pass over γ + 1 positions takes about as long as one target call; c is the time of a draft call divided by a target call. It predicts shapes, not benchmarks.

    Takeaway

    Drafting pays off when α is high and c is small — and it can be slower.

    11Putting it together

    Same distribution, a different schedule.

    Back to the dilemma of Part I. One request, drawn both ways on the same time axis as in the KV Cache Lab. Each tick is a token reaching the user: standard decoding ticks evenly, draft-and-verify delivers tokens in bursts.

    Read the timeline

    Time is counted in target-call units (one cached decode step = 1). Prefill is the same in both lanes, so the first token is not faster.

    Takeaway

    Speed comes from a second model — never from a different answer.

    What stays the same?the output distribution

    Tokens are still samples from the target model, with its temperature, top-k or top-p settings.

    What gets faster?fewer target calls

    Decode time, when the draft is cheap and usually right. Prefill and time-to-first-token do not improve.

    What does it cost?a second model

    Draft compute, its own KV cache in memory, wasted work after a rejection, and burstier streaming.
